# Twitter/X Thread Templates

Brand-aligned thread structures for Fabrica. Swap the bracketed fields with a
real workflow, real evidence, and a real outcome. Keep the voice: direct,
builder-first, anti-hype. Never invent metrics, quotes, or customers (see
`brand/brand-guidelines.md` §5).

## How to use these templates

- **Hook first.** Tweet 1 must stop the scroll. Name a specific, painful reality
  in the first two lines.
- **One job per tweet.** Each tweet earns the next one. If a tweet can be cut and
  the thread still works, cut it.
- **Lead with the outcome, then the mechanism.** "You stop being the bottleneck"
  before "agents run in isolated worktrees."
- **End threads with a CTA** that is concrete, not "follow for more."
- **Formatting:** a strong opening line, a line break, then the body. Keep tweets
  under 240 characters so they retweet cleanly. Use sparing punctuation emphasis,
  not all caps.
- **Visuals:** Forge Dark background, one concise claim, one product/workflow
  visual, one Molten Orange focal point. Keep the lower-right corner clear.
- **Blacklist:** no "AI-powered," "revolutionary," "game-changing," "seamless,"
  "effortless," or "magic." No unverified "10x" claims.

## 2. How-To Thread (8 tweets)

**When to use:** teach one repeatable workflow end to end. Establishes Fabrica
as the thing you actually run your work on.

### Tweet 1 — Hook

> Most "automation" fails for one boring reason: nobody reviews the work.
>
> Here's the 6-step workflow we use to ship a piece of work on Fabrica —
> from brief to output, without losing judgment. 👇

### Tweet 2 — Step 0: Pick one job

> Step 0: pick ONE job you'd normally do yourself. Not a whole department.
> One job with a clear finish line.
>
> Example: "Audit our onboarding funnel and recommend three fixes."
>
> If the job can't be described in one sentence, it's too big. Break it up.

### Tweet 3 — Step 1: Write the brief

> Step 1: write the brief like you'd brief a contractor.
>
> → the outcome you want
> → the context they need
> → what "done" looks like
> → what to do if it hits a dead end
>
> A good brief is 80% of a good output. This is the step people skip, then
> blame the agent.

### Tweet 4 — Step 2: Assign it

> Step 2: assign the brief to a specialist agent.
>
> Researcher. Developer. Marketer. Analyst.
>
> It runs in parallel with your other work, in its own isolated workspace —
> so nothing collides with what you're building right now.

### Tweet 5 — Step 3: Review the plan

> Step 3: before anything expensive runs, Fabrica shows you the plan.
>
> Read it. Fix the brief if the plan is wrong. This is your first control point,
> and it costs 30 seconds.

### Tweet 6 — Step 4: Approve and run

> Step 4: approve the run. Work executes inside the guardrails you set —
> the budget line and the checkpoints.
>
> You're not watching it. You're not micromanaging it. You set the boundaries,
> and the work moves inside them.

### Tweet 7 — Step 5: Review the output, then decide

> Step 5: the output lands in a review queue, not your email.
>
> Same test you'd apply to any contractor: does it match the brief? Is the
> context there? Would you ship it under your name?
>
> Approve. Rework. Or reject. The call stays yours.

### Tweet 8 — Wrap-up / CTA

> That's the whole system:
>
> Brief → assign → review the plan → approve → run → review the output.
>
> Every step visible. Every consequential move yours to make.
>
> Try it on one job this week. [Link] We'll help you pick the first one.

**Adapt:** replace the example job ("Audit our onboarding funnel…") with your
current product workflow and swap the agent names to match what you show.

## 3. Founder Story Thread (8 tweets)

**When to use:** build trust, humanize the product, and explain *why* Fabrica
exists. Candid, learning-in-public voice. No invented details — tell the true
story or leave blanks marked [ ].

### Tweet 1 — Hook

> We didn't build Fabrica to make another AI tool.
>
> We built it because we watched ourselves become the bottleneck of our own
> company — and we hated the two options everyone offered us.
> 👇

### Tweet 2 — The setup

> Like every founder, we had five jobs at once:
>
> → product
> → code
> → marketing
> → fundraising
> → actually talking to customers
>
> And the "solution" everyone sold us was: give the AI a task and walk away.

### Tweet 3 — The turning point

> We tried it. It didn't break, and that was the problem.
>
> An agent could do a task. But we couldn't see what it was doing, couldn't
> stop it at the moment it mattered, and couldn't trust an output with no
> context attached.
>
> Autonomy without visibility isn't leverage. It's a gamble.

### Tweet 4 — The gap

> The real gap wasn't "can AI do the work?"
>
> It was: can a founder direct the work, see it moving, and keep the final call?
>
> Every tool was one or the other. A chat with a genie, or a black box running
> on its own. Neither is a business.

### Tweet 5 — What we started building

> So we started building the thing we wanted: a command center.
>
> Brief a specialist. Watch the work move. Approve the consequential steps.
> Stop it at a budget line. Bring your own models so the spend is yours.
>
> Agents as a crew with jobs. Not a genie.

### Tweet 6 — What we learned the hard way

> The honest part: control is harder to design than autonomy.
>
> The first versions were either too cautious — everything stopped, nothing moved —
> or too loose — things ran past where we were comfortable.
>
> Approval gates and budget stops aren't features. They're the product.

### Tweet 7 — What changed

> Once the control points were right, everything changed.
>
> We stopped being the person who has to touch every task. We became the person
> who decides what moves — and reviews what matters.
>
> That's the whole promise: your business, automated. Your judgment, still in
> the loop.

### Tweet 8 — CTA

> Fabrica is in early access, and we're still finding what breaks. We'd rather
> you tell us where it's rough than pretend it's finished.
>
> [Link]
>
> Give it one real workflow. If it doesn't earn its keep, tell us. We read
> every reply.

**Adapt:** keep the beats (setup → turning point → gap → build → lesson → change →
CTA) and fill in the true specifics. Do not invent quotes, dates, or metrics.

## 4. Hot Take Thread (7 tweets)

**When to use:** stake a position, start a conversation, interrupt a category
assumption. The take must be defensible and grounded — this is founder-led
opinion, not clickbait.

### Tweet 1 — The take

> "AI agents will run your company" is the wrong goal.
>
> The right goal: AI agents run the work while you keep the judgment.
>
> Every company that treats "autonomy" as the endgame is about to learn why
> humans approve things. 👇

### Tweet 2 — Why the pitch is wrong

> The pitch sounds great: "set it and forget it."
>
> Here's what it actually means in practice:
>
> → no one reviews what shipped
> → no one catches the wrong context
> → no one stops the run before it spends
>
> You didn't remove the bottleneck. You moved it to the incident report.

### Tweet 3 — What autonomy is really for

> Autonomy is good at the boring, bounded parts:
>
> → a research pass
> → a first draft
> → a code change with clear scope
>
> That's where agents earn their keep. Those jobs don't need a founder's eyes
> on every line. They need a gate at the end.

### Tweet 4 — Where it goes wrong

> The dangerous part is the middle of the work, not the start.
>
> That's where context gets lost, budgets drift, and a confident agent invents
> a detail to fill the gap.
>
> If nobody can see it while it's moving, you don't find out until it's done.

### Tweet 5 — The fix isn't less AI

> So the answer isn't "no agents." That's just slower.
>
> The answer is structure:
>
> → a clear brief
> → visible status while it runs
> → a review point before consequential moves
> → a hard budget stop
>
> That's not fear of automation. That's how you get to trust it.

### Tweet 6 — What we're building toward

> This is exactly what we're building at Fabrica.
>
> A command center where agents run in parallel and founders keep the call:
> review the plan, approve the run, check the budget line.
>
> We want useful work to move without you becoming the bottleneck. Not without
> you being in charge.

### Tweet 7 — Prompt + CTA

> Agree or not — the question is worth answering:
>
> Where is the line between "delegating work" and "handing over judgment"?
>
> Tell us where you draw it. [Link] And if you want a system that keeps you on
> the judgment side of that line, here's what we're building.

**Adapt:** replace the take with your own defensible position, but keep the
structure: claim → why the category is wrong → where autonomy is real → where it
breaks → the fix → what you're building → a question that invites replies.
